SETD5 Syndrome Foundation · Working Draft

Strategic framework

Draft for board discussion, not for adoption
The Foundation operates across three arms: Research, Community, and Communication. Each arm has a defined scope, and each supports the others. The goal is a credible, unified organization that the SETD5 Syndrome community can trust.
Strategic purpose

Why this Foundation exists

The SETD5 Syndrome Foundation exists to support families living with SETD5 Syndrome and to advance research that improves their lives.

In this early stage, the Foundation's priority is to build the systems, relationships, and trust needed for long-term impact. This framework is intended to help the board discuss how the Foundation can grow thoughtfully, responsibly, and in alignment with its mission.

How to read this framework

The three arms are functions, not formal committees

The three arms (Research, Community, and Communication) describe the Foundation's major areas of work. They don't need to become separate standing committees immediately. This framework is meant to help organize discussion, clarify priorities, and identify what governance processes need to be created as the Foundation develops.

The framework

The three arms

Each arm describes a major area of the Foundation's work. They are not separate silos; they overlap, inform each other, and ultimately serve the same mission.

Community Community arm
Objective

Welcome newly diagnosed families, help families find existing resources, create opportunities for connection, and listen to the community so future Foundation programs are shaped by actual family needs.

Key activities
  • Navigation and resource guidance for newly diagnosed families
  • Help families find existing resources, including the SETD5 Syndrome Companion Resource Hub and the existing independent Facebook group, while making clear that those resources remain separate from the Foundation
  • Serve as a welcoming navigation point for families rather than duplicating existing resource libraries or independent community spaces
  • Foundation Facebook page for announcements about research opportunities and community events
  • Introduction Zoom meetup to welcome new families to the Foundation
  • Regional Zoom meetup series (meet and greet format)
  • Family needs survey to identify what the community needs most
  • Monthly newsletter, produced by the Communication arm, with each arm contributing content: Research provides updates on studies and opportunities, Community provides family stories and event recaps, and Communication handles editing, consistency, and distribution.
  • Contribute a "Community Corner" to the monthly newsletter: a regular feature that tells the story of a family living with SETD5 Syndrome. The Community arm is responsible for identifying families, obtaining consent, and gathering content, which is then passed to the Communication arm for editing and inclusion.
  • In-person events (future growth phase)
Progression

Start with online channels and announcements, build relationships through virtual meetups, and expand to in-person events when the Foundation has the capacity to support them.

Family survey and privacy considerations
Any family needs survey should include a clear purpose, a privacy statement, an explanation of how responses will be used, and a plan for sharing summarized findings back with the community. The survey should be designed to listen to families, identify gaps, and guide Foundation priorities without collecting more personal information than necessary.
Guiding questions
  • What does a newly diagnosed family need from us in their first contact with the Foundation?
  • How do we direct families to existing resources without making them feel passed off?
  • What should regional Zoom meetups look like, and what cadence makes sense given our capacity?
  • How do we identify gaps in community needs that existing resources don't fill?
  • What should our family needs survey ask, and when is the right time to send it?
  • How do we make sure the survey reaches families who aren't yet connected to our existing channels?
  • What do we do with the survey findings, and how do they inform the Foundation's priorities?
  • What measures help us know whether we're serving families well?
  • When and how do we move from virtual to in-person community events?
  • What should our introduction Zoom meetup look like, and who should be invited?
  • How will families be told what information is being collected and how it will be used?
  • What consent is needed before sharing family stories, photos, or quotes publicly?
  • How do we make sure families feel invited and supported, not pressured to participate in programs, surveys, or research?
Approach to community building

The Foundation's role is to welcome families, help them find what's already available, and identify gaps that may need to be addressed over time. The Foundation doesn't need to duplicate existing resources or absorb independent community spaces in order to be useful. Families should feel welcomed, understood, and clearly directed to high-quality resources.

Starting with announcements and virtual meetups allows the Foundation to build relationships and trust before scaling to in-person events. It also creates an opportunity to listen to families about what they actually need, which will inform future work across all three arms.

Key relationship

Relationships with independent community leaders should be handled with care. The Foundation should position itself as a navigation point and coordinating presence, not as a replacement for independent community spaces. A simple shared understanding with independent spaces can help prevent confusion, duplicated efforts, or unnecessary tension later.

Volunteering

Volunteer engagement requires structure, but the Foundation should be careful not to be so restrictive that it turns away the families it exists to serve. Parents who want to help and are told there is nothing for them often don't stay quiet; they feel excluded from an organization built because of their children. That kind of early friction is hard to undo.

A practical approach uses two tracks. The first is active recruitment into defined roles: project-based (a specific deliverable with a clear end point) or ongoing (a recurring function requiring consistent engagement). Both require a written role description, a clear point of contact, and a screening process before anyone is brought in. No one should be placed in an active volunteer role without the Foundation being ready to receive them.

The second track is a volunteer interest pipeline: a lightweight way to capture enthusiasm before roles exist. Someone completes a short interest form, shares relevant skills and availability, and the Foundation responds with "we'll reach out when something fits." No commitment is made on either side, no management burden is created, and no one is turned away. When a need arises, the Foundation already has a pool to draw from. This is also how you identify parents with professional skills (lawyers, scientists, grant writers, marketers) before you know exactly how to use them.

The goal is to make sure every parent who wants to help is welcomed, not turned away. The interest form exists precisely for this: any board member can and should direct enthusiastic parents to it at any time. The process question is narrower — placing someone in an active role with real responsibilities and a point of contact should happen through a defined process so the Foundation is ready to receive them well.

  • What active volunteer roles, if any, does the Foundation need in its first year, and which arm owns them?
  • Who has authority to activate a volunteer into a defined role, and what process is required before that happens?
  • What does our volunteer interest pipeline look like, and how do we follow up with people in it?
  • What is our screening process, and does it differ for volunteers who will have contact with families or access to Foundation materials?
  • What guidelines do volunteers follow when representing the Foundation publicly?
Research Research arm
Objective

Build and maintain relationships with the medical and scientific community, support the development of a Scientific and Medical Advisory Board, and create responsible processes for evaluating research and medical guidance opportunities that may benefit the SETD5 Syndrome community.

Key activities
  • Establish a Scientific Advisory Board (potentially combined with a Medical Advisory Board initially)
  • Support the Medical Advisory Board in developing family-facing medical screening or surveillance guidance for SETD5 Syndrome, with the Foundation serving as the organizer, publisher, and family-facing communication partner rather than the medical author; this work does not begin until a functioning MAB is in place
  • Identify and vet research partnership opportunities, including natural history studies. The Foundation has already been approached by a researcher seeking to fund a natural history study and requesting dissemination support to families, making this an active near-term priority
  • Create clear vetting protocols for research partnerships and advisory board candidates (see SAB candidate vetting checklist)
Foundational phases
  1. Build relationships with researchers, clinicians, and scientific organizations.
  2. Identify potential Scientific and Medical Advisory Board candidates.
  3. Establish formal criteria and processes for evaluating research partnerships, advisory board candidates, and medical guidance projects.
  4. Clarify which research-related decisions require full board approval, advisory input, or officer/designated lead action.
Medical guidance clarification
Any medical screening, surveillance, or care guidance shared by the Foundation should be developed or reviewed by qualified medical professionals through the Medical Advisory Board. The Foundation's role is to support access, clarity, and dissemination, not to independently create medical recommendations.
Guiding questions
  • Who should serve on our Scientific and Medical Advisory Board?
  • What criteria and red lines define a partnership we'll support versus one we won't?
  • How do we ensure consistency across board members so decisions aren't made unilaterally?
  • What does our formal vetting process look like for research partnerships?
  • How do we balance being responsive to researchers with having proper governance in place?
  • Is the research broad-based or niche, and does that change our level of involvement?
  • What level of Foundation involvement is being requested: dissemination, funding, staff time, or use of our name?
  • Does the study have proper ethics oversight and sound study design?
Shared with Community and Communication
  • How will the Foundation protect family privacy when sharing research opportunities?
  • What consent is needed before sharing family stories, photos, survey findings, or family-provided information with researchers or the public?
  • How do we make clear that families should never feel pressured to participate in research?
Vetting framework for research partnerships

A vetting framework means establishing clear, consistent criteria for evaluating research opportunities. The level of scrutiny should match the level of Foundation involvement being requested.

Level of involvement Approach
Low: dissemination only More permissive. Share with families as long as basic ethics and credibility checks pass.
Medium: name attached, promotion Evaluate scope, researcher credibility, and alignment with community needs.
High: funding, staff time, full endorsement Rigorous review required. Assess whether research serves the broad SETD5 Syndrome community, not just a narrow subset. Review funding sources, conflicts of interest, study design, and ethics oversight.

This vetting table is a starting framework for board discussion. It should be treated as a living document that can be refined once the Scientific and Medical Advisory Board is formed and as the Foundation gains experience reviewing research opportunities.

Conflict of interest disclosure: Nonprofit governance best practice requires that any board member with a personal, professional, or financial relationship to a researcher, institution, or opportunity under review disclose that relationship before the board discusses or votes on the matter, and recuse themselves from the decision. This applies to all research partnership decisions, advisory board appointments, and formal endorsements. Board members have already signed a conflict of interest policy as part of Foundation governance.
Key vetting criteria
  • Researcher track record and credibility
  • Funding sources and potential conflicts of interest
  • Study design and scientific rigor, including ethics oversight
  • Scope: does it represent the broader SETD5 Syndrome community or a narrow subset?
  • Alignment with Foundation values and community benefit
  • Level of Foundation involvement being requested
Process components
  • Formal application or inquiry process
  • Defined review timeline
  • Clear decision-making authority: full board, subcommittee, or advisory board
Approach to early research partnerships

When approached by a researcher, the Foundation can acknowledge the opportunity, express genuine interest, and explain that formal commitments will need to wait until appropriate governance, advisory, and vetting processes are in place.

Example message "We're genuinely interested in learning more about this opportunity and appreciate your interest in the SETD5 Syndrome community. Because we are still in the early stages as a Foundation, we are putting thoughtful governance and advisory processes in place so that any research collaboration is handled responsibly and consistently. We would be glad to schedule an introductory conversation to better understand the scope of the study, the type of Foundation involvement being requested, and any ethics or institutional review already in place. From there, we can identify what next steps would make sense as our advisory and review processes develop."
For board discussion: Frame vetting criteria not as barriers to opportunity, but as the infrastructure that protects the Foundation's credibility and the community's trust. Asking researchers to wait while the Foundation formalizes its process isn't losing an opportunity. It demonstrates that the Foundation is a trustworthy partner worth waiting for.
Communication Communication arm
Objective

Ensure consistent messaging, branding, and organizational voice across Foundation channels so families, researchers, donors, and partners experience the Foundation as clear, credible, and trustworthy.

Key activities
  • Develop and document organizational messaging pillars
  • Establish visual branding consistency across the website, Facebook, and social media
  • Create organizational voice guidelines
  • Align communication across Foundation channels before significant research recruitment, public campaigns, or large-scale community outreach begins
  • Develop branded print and digital materials: flyers, business cards, and reusable templates for events, outreach, and partner communications
  • Produce a monthly newsletter that keeps families, researchers, and supporters informed about Foundation news, research updates, and community events. Content is gathered from the Research and Community arms, with the Communication arm responsible for editing, consistency, and distribution.
Guiding questions
  • What are our key messaging pillars: the three to five ideas we want people to understand about the Foundation?
  • What's our organizational voice and tone, and how does it differ from clinical or academic language while still being credible?
  • How do we represent the Foundation consistently across all platforms?
  • Who are our primary audiences (newly diagnosed families, researchers, donors, the general public), and how does our messaging differ for each?
  • How do we ensure all board members, staff, and volunteers communicate with one voice?
  • Who represents the Foundation at various events, and what are their communication guidelines?
  • What format should the monthly newsletter take, and what process do the Research and Community arms use to submit content?
  • Who has final editorial authority over the newsletter before it goes out?
  • What platform will we use to distribute the newsletter, and how do families subscribe?
Approach

Before significant research recruitment, public campaigns, or large-scale community outreach begins, the Foundation should define who it is and what it stands for. Public-facing materials should be aligned before the Foundation asks families, researchers, donors, or partners to take action. Messaging pillars, organizational voice, and visual guidelines should help the website, Facebook page, social media, and printed materials feel like they come from one coherent organization.

When communication is aligned from the start, every public interaction reinforces the Foundation's identity and builds credibility with both families and researchers. Formal introductions to external organizations should not begin until the website is live.

Cross-cutting areas

Areas that span all three arms

The following areas don't belong to a single arm — they support and cut across all three. Each one requires the board to establish shared expectations before significant work begins.

Cross-arm
Outreach
Research Community Communication

Building and maintaining relationships with external organizations is an activity that spans all three arms. The Foundation should establish clear, consistent expectations around these relationships — not to create gatekeeping, but to protect credibility, prevent conflicting commitments, and make sure the right people are informed at the right time.

Formal introductions to external organizations should wait until the Foundation website is live. The website establishes the Foundation's credibility and gives contacts somewhere to learn more before a conversation begins. Reaching out before the site is ready puts the Foundation at a disadvantage.
Managing external relationships
  • The President is the default point of contact for first introductions and formal applications, unless the board designates someone else.
  • The arm most relevant to the relationship should support the substance of the outreach — Research for research contacts, Community for family organizations, Communication for media and partner networks.
  • Any relationship that involves fees, signed agreements, data sharing, public commitments, policy positions, or use of the Foundation's name should come to the board for review before moving forward.
  • Once a relationship is established, the board can assign an ongoing liaison. That person does not have to be the President.

Development and sustainability

Fundraising and financial sustainability support all three arms of the Foundation. At this early stage, the board should discuss what the Foundation can responsibly raise money for, what expenses are most urgent, and what financial controls should be in place before larger fundraising campaigns begin.

Because early-stage infrastructure can be difficult for donors to understand, the board may eventually need one or two concrete, mission-aligned projects that make the Foundation's infrastructure visible as impact. One possible first-year anchor project could be a community needs assessment, such as "The State of SETD5 Syndrome Families." This kind of project could listen to families, identify community needs, inform future research and programming priorities, and create a credible communication piece for partners and donors.

  • What are the Foundation's first-year funding needs?
  • What can the Foundation responsibly fund before 501(c)(3) confirmation, if applicable?
  • What donor messaging aligns with the Foundation's mission and early-stage capacity?
  • Will early fundraising support research, family support, education, operations, or some combination?
  • What financial controls, approval processes, and reporting practices should be in place before fundraising campaigns begin?
Governance and decision-making

As the Foundation develops, the board should clarify which decisions require full board approval, which can be handled by an officer or designated lead within approved guidelines, and which should be advisory only.

Suggested starting point for board discussion:

Decision area Suggested decision path
Mission statement and public messaging pillars Full board approval
Routine social media posts Communications lead or officer within approved guidelines
Research endorsement or formal Foundation partnership Full board approval, with advisory input when available
Initial screening and vetting of research opportunities Research lead or officer conducts initial review; full board approval required for formal endorsement or partnership
Research Advisory Board and Scientific Advisory Board candidate vetting and invitations Research lead or officer vets candidates and presents to the board for approval; research team extends formal invitations
Routine research committee communications and meeting scheduling Research lead or officer within approved guidelines
Family meetup scheduling Community lead or officer within approved guidelines
Family needs survey content and data-use language Board review or board-approved process
Use of Foundation name, logo, or endorsement Full board approval or board-approved process
Red line / heightened review: sharing identifiable family data, family stories, photos, medical information, survey responses, or unpublished community findings with a third party Full board approval, documented consent, and legal or ethics review when appropriate

This table is a starting point for discussion and doesn't imply the board has already adopted these decision paths.

This category protects families, the Foundation, and the trust needed for future research and community work.

Saying yes to an opportunity before a process is in place can create problems even when the opportunity is genuinely good. Consistency protects the Foundation's credibility and the community's trust. When the Foundation follows its own processes, it demonstrates that its decisions are principled, not reactive.

First three to six months
First three to six month priorities

The Foundation does not need to build every program at once. In the first three to six months, the priority should be to establish the structure, language, and processes needed for responsible growth.

Early priorities may include:

  • Finalize mission language, messaging pillars, and voice and visual guidelines.
  • Establish research partnership vetting criteria.
  • Identify potential Scientific and Medical Advisory Board candidates.
  • Clarify the process for developing or reviewing family-facing medical guidance through the Medical Advisory Board; actual guidance development begins once the MAB is in place.
  • Create a simple welcome and navigation pathway for newly diagnosed families.
  • Join key rare disease organizations.
  • Draft volunteer role descriptions, expectations, and screening guidelines.
  • Clarify decision-making authority for research, communications, community activities, and use of the Foundation name.

Later growth areas may include:

  • In-person events.
  • Structured peer support, once facilitation protocols and crisis procedures are in place.
  • Larger research funding decisions.
  • Broader policy or advocacy involvement.
  • More developed family programs.
